# Cognitive-Motor Training Technology Evaluation Framework

**Version 1.0 · CMT Systems Methodology**

This framework gives clinics, senior living organizations, schools, and sports performance facilities a consistent way to understand, evaluate, and compare cognitive-motor training technologies before making an equipment investment.

**Important distinction:** This is an equipment evaluation and purchasing framework—not a validated clinical assessment, diagnostic tool, or guarantee of treatment outcomes.

## The Five Buyer Questions

The framework is designed to answer five fundamental questions:

1. **What cognitive and physical activities can the technology support?**
2. **How configurable are those activities?**
3. **What does the participant actually have to do?**
4. **What information does the system measure or report?**
5. **Does the equipment fit the organization's people, space, workflow, and objectives?**

## Framework Principles

The framework follows six rules:

1. **Evaluate capabilities, not brand reputation.** A recognizable manufacturer name does not establish suitability.
2. **Separate cognitive demands from physical demands.** A system may offer complex cognitive activities but limited movement options, or vice versa.
3. **Evaluate configurability.** The ability to adjust timing, stimuli, response rules, and movement demands affects how an activity is used.
4. **Distinguish data from outcomes.** A recorded score or response time is a system metric; it is not automatically proof of clinical improvement.
5. **Evaluate the complete implementation.** Space, staffing, training, accessibility, cost, and support matter alongside software and equipment.
6. **Disclose commercial relationships.** Recommendations must distinguish independent evaluation from compensated sales or referrals.

## The Five Evaluation Dimensions

### 01 · Cognitive Complexity

**Task demands:** What kinds of cognitive activities can the system present, and how can their complexity be adjusted?

**What to assess:**
- Attention
- Visual scanning
- Processing speed
- Inhibitory control
- Working memory
- Decision-making
- Task switching
- Language-based activities

**Key questions:**
- Which cognitive activities are built into the system?
- Can users create or customize activities?
- Can difficulty be adjusted independently of physical demands?
- Can the system progress from simple stimulus recognition to more complex rules?

### 02 · Stimulus Flexibility

**Input design:** What does the participant see or hear, and how configurable are the stimuli?

**What to assess:**
- Stimulus type: colors, shapes, symbols, letters, numbers, words, equations
- Stimulus quantity: single or multiple active targets
- Stimulus location: fixed, sequential, randomized, or spatially distributed
- Presentation timing: duration, frequency, speed, and intervals
- Customization: whether users can create or upload content

**Key questions:**
- Can the system present multiple types of visual stimuli?
- Can stimulus quantity be changed?
- Can the organization customize content for its intended activities?

### 03 · Response Flexibility

**Response rules:** How does the participant respond, and how much decision-making does the task require?

**What to assess:**
- Touch, strike, reach, select, or other response methods
- Single-response and multiple-response formats
- Hit-versus-avoid rules
- Left-versus-right response requirements
- Sequential responses
- Response inhibition

**Key questions:**
- Does the system support more than one response method?
- Can response rules be changed?
- Can participants be asked to avoid certain stimuli?
- Can response demands be scaled without changing the entire activity?

### 04 · Physical Movement Integration

**Movement demands:** How does the system incorporate physical movement into cognitive activities?

**What to assess:**
- Upper-body movement: reaching, striking, bilateral movement
- Lower-body movement: stepping, directional movement, foot responses
- Whole-body movement: multiple movement patterns across a larger area
- Balance-related demands: whether tasks can be combined with balance-related activity
- Spatial demand: how much floor space and movement clearance are needed
- Adaptability: whether movement requirements can be modified

**Key questions:**
- Does the system support upper-body, lower-body, or whole-body activities?
- Can cognitive and physical demands be adjusted separately?
- Can the equipment accommodate seated and standing activities, if required?
- What movement range is necessary?

**Important:** The presence of a balance-related activity does not establish that the equipment prevents falls or improves balance outcomes. Those are separate clinical claims requiring appropriate evidence.

### 05 · Performance Data Capabilities

**Measurement:** What information does the system collect, display, store, and export?

**What to assess:**
- Response time
- Accuracy
- Score
- Completion time
- Session history
- Participant profiles
- Performance trends
- Data export
- Reporting functionality
- User permissions and data management

**Key questions:**
- What metrics are collected automatically?
- Which metrics are available for individual activities?
- Are results available in real time?
- Can users review previous sessions?
- Can data be exported?
- What do the metrics actually represent?

**Important:** A response-time metric may be useful for observing performance within a particular task. It should not be described as a validated measure of overall cognitive function unless that use has been independently established.

## Standard Scoring Rubric

Each dimension receives a score from 1 to 5. These are proposed CMT Systems evaluation scores, not clinical ratings or industry certifications.

| Score | Rating    | Interpretation                                     |
| ----- | --------- | -------------------------------------------------- |
| 1     | Limited   | Little documented capability in this dimension     |
| 2     | Basic     | Some functionality, with significant limitations   |
| 3     | Moderate  | Useful capabilities for defined applications       |
| 4     | Advanced  | Broad capabilities with meaningful configuration   |
| 5     | Extensive | Extensive documented functionality and flexibility |

**Use N/V (Not Verified)** when information is insufficient.  
**Use N/A (Not Applicable)** when a feature is genuinely irrelevant to the intended application.

Never assign a high score merely because a manufacturer advertises a capability.

## Evidence and Verification Standard

Every statement in a comparison should be traceable to an evidence source.

| Evidence level                     | Source                                                                     | How to present it                                              |
| ---------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- |
| A — Independent research           | Peer-reviewed research relevant to the claim                               | Explain study context, population, limitations and applicability |
| B — Independent technical evidence | Testing, technical documentation, or independently verified specifications | State what was tested and how                                    |
| C — Manufacturer documentation     | Official manuals, specifications and product materials                     | Attribute the information to the manufacturer                    |
| D — Demonstration                  | Direct observation or demonstration                                        | Describe what was observed, not what is universally possible     |
| E — Unverified claim               | Sales statements or unsupported assertions                                 | Do not present as established fact                               |

### Evidence Rules

**Rule 1: Capability is not efficacy.**  
A system that offers a Stroop-style task can be described as offering a task involving conflicting stimulus information and response rules. That alone does not prove improvement in executive function.

**Rule 2: A metric is not a clinical outcome.**  
Response time, accuracy, and score may describe task performance. They should not automatically be interpreted as measures of functional independence, fall risk, memory health, or rehabilitation progress.

**Rule 3: Research must match the claim.**  
A study involving one population, intervention, and outcome cannot automatically support claims for every population or equipment platform.

**Rule 4: Transfer claims need separate evidence.**  
Improvement on a trained task does not, by itself, demonstrate transfer to untrained activities or real-world functional outcomes.

**Rule 5: Disclose uncertainty.**  
If evidence is limited, conflicting, manufacturer-funded, or not directly relevant to the equipment being evaluated, say so.
