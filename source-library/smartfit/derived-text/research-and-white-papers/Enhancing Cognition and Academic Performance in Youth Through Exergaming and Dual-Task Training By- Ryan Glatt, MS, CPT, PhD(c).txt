Enhancing Cognition and 
Academic Performance in Children 
Through Exergaming and Dual-Task 
Training 
By: Ryan Glatt, MS, CPT. PhD(c). 
Sponsored by: SMARTﬁt Inc. 
https://smartﬁtinc.com/lp/after-school-
programs/ 
Introduction 
Cognitive development and academic 
success in children are heavily influenced 
by executive functions (EFs), which 
encompass cognitive flexibility, inhibitory 
control, and attention. Recent research 
suggests that incorporating physical 
activity into cognitive training, particularly 
through exergaming and dual-task 
training (DTT), offers substantial benefits 
for cognitive performance and academic 
achievement. Exergaming, a form of 
physical activity that integrates 
interactive video gaming, and DTT, which 
requires individuals to engage in 
simultaneous cognitive and motor tasks, 
have both been associated with 
improvements in executive function, 
postural control, and learning outcomes 
(Kou et al., 2024; Wollesen et al., 2022). 
The Impact of Exergaming on Cognition 
Exergaming has been shown to enhance 
various executive functions, including 
cognitive flexibility, inhibitory control, and 
global cognition. A systematic review and 
meta-analysis conducted by Kou et al. 
(2024) found that exergaming significantly 
improved cognitive flexibility (SMD = 
0.34), inhibitory control (SMD = 0.51), and 
global cognition (SMD = 0.87) in children. 
These cognitive enhancements translate 
into better academic performance, as 
executive functions play a critical role in 
attentional control, working memory, and 
problem-solving abilities. Additionally, 
studies have highlighted the positive 
effects of cognitively engaging movement 
games on academic achievement. Chou 
et al. (2023) demonstrated that 
movement-based cognitive tasks 
significantly improved interference 
control, a crucial EF for learning, in 
overweight children. Similarly, 
Kolovelonis et al. (2023) found that acute 
exergaming sessions in elementary 
physical education classes enhanced 
students' executive functions and 
situational interest in learning activities. 
Dual-Task Training and Cognitive 
Development 
Dual-task training (DTT) involves 
performing cognitive and motor tasks 
simultaneously, which has been shown to 
improve both cognitive and motor 
performance. A scoping review by 
Wollesen et al. (2022) highlighted that 
DTT interventions enhance executive 
functions and motor coordination in 
children and adolescents. These benefits 
are particularly notable in populations 
with cognitive and motor deficits, such as 
adolescents with Down syndrome. Triki et 
al. (2024) found that an eight-week DTT 
program significantly improved both 
postural control and cognitive 
performance in adolescents with Down 
syndrome, demonstrating the 
intervention's potential for enhancing 
functional abilities. Furthermore, 
Bustillo-Casero et al. (2020) reported that 
DTT led to greater improvements in dual-
task conditions compared to single-task

training (STT), reinforcing the importance 
of integrated cognitive-motor training. 
Age-Related Differences in Dual-Task 
Training Benefits 
Research suggests that older children 
benefit more from dual-task training due 
to their more developed executive 
functions. Möhring et al. (2021) found that 
children aged 8-13 demonstrated better 
dual-task performance than younger 
children, particularly in updating and 
switching tasks. Similarly, Cowan et al. 
(2021) observed that children aged 10-14 
showed greater attention allocation and 
cognitive control when balancing two 
simultaneous tasks compared to children 
aged 6-8. Yang et al. (2017) documented 
a continuous linear improvement in 
multitasking abilities from ages 7 to 12, 
indicating that as children age, their 
cognitive and motor skills develop 
further, making them more adept at dual-
tasking. 
Training Duration and Frequency 
The duration and frequency of dual-task 
training significantly impact cognitive 
outcomes in children, particularly in 
enhancing executive functions and 
academic performance. Longer 
interventions tend to yield greater 
cognitive benefits, with studies showing 
that a nine-week coordination training 
program incorporating mental tasks 
resulted in significant improvements in 
both motor and cognitive functions in 
children aged 6-10 (Santner et al., 2018). 
Additionally, an eight-week DTT 
intervention improved postural and 
cognitive performance in adolescents 
with Down syndrome (Triki et al., 2024). 
Chen et al. (2021) conducted a meta-
analysis on cognitive interventions in 
children with ADHD and found that higher 
training frequency was associated with 
greater executive function improvements. 
Similarly, Johann and Karbach (2020) 
demonstrated that 21 sessions of 
executive control training led to 
significant gains in academic 
performance, particularly in reading and 
mathematical skills. 
Comparing Dual-Task Training to 
Traditional Cognitive Training 
While traditional cognitive training 
methods focus primarily on cognitive 
enhancements, dual-task training offers a 
more holistic approach by integrating 
cognitive and motor skills. DTT has been 
found to be more effective in improving 
executive functions and academic 
performance than STT (Wollesen et al., 
2022). Triki et al. (2024) demonstrated 
that dual-task interventions produced 
greater improvements in postural control 
and cognitive performance than 
traditional cognitive exercises. Bustillo-
Casero et al. (2020) reported that 
adolescents participating in DTT exhibited 
superior motor-cognitive integration, 
which is critical for real-world problem-
solving. On the other hand, traditional 
cognitive training methods, such as 
game-based executive control training, 
have also shown benefits. Johann and 
Karbach (2020) found that game-based 
cognitive training increased motivation 
and domain-specific cognitive 
improvements, particularly in reading 
performance. However, dual-task training 
provides a broader range of benefits by 
simultaneously enhancing motor 
coordination and cognitive functions,

making it a more effective strategy for 
holistic cognitive development. 
Conclusion 
Exergaming and dual-task training offer 
promising interventions for enhancing 
cognition and academic performance in 
children. By integrating physical activity 
with cognitive engagement, these 
approaches improve executive functions, 
including cognitive flexibility, inhibitory 
control, and attention. Older children and 
adolescents benefit the most from DTT, 
as their executive functions are more 
developed. Additionally, longer and more 
frequent training sessions yield greater 
cognitive improvements. While traditional 
cognitive training methods remain 
beneficial, dual-task training provides 
more comprehensive benefits by 
enhancing both cognitive and motor 
skills, making it a superior approach for 
academic success. Future educational 
programs should incorporate these 
strategies to optimize cognitive 
development in children.
