The Benefits of Dual-Task 
Training in Military Personnel 
By: Ryan Glatt, MS, CPT, PhD(c) 
Sponsored by: SMARTfit Inc. 
Dual-task training which involves the 
simultaneous practice of a cognitive and 
motor task, offers potential benefits for 
military personnel by enhancing their ability 
to perform in real-world multitasking 
environments. This approach may 
demonstrate improvements in cognitive-
motor interference, postural stability, 
operational readiness post-traumatic 
brain injury (TBI), and performance under 
stress. Moreover, it can enhance key 
executive functions such as working 
memory, cognitive flexibility, task 
coordination, and attentional control, 
possibly making it more effective than 
single-task training. 
Dual-task training can address cognitive-
motor interference, a phenomenon where 
performance declines when cognitive and 
motor tasks are performed simultaneously. 
This training can improve the ability to 
manage interference, potentially leading to 
enhanced performance in both domains. 
Gattoni et al. (2023) demonstrated that 
dual-task training may improve gait 
stability and obstacle clearance while 
performing cognitive tasks, highlighting its 
relevance for multitasking military 
operations. Postural stability is critical for 
military personnel who often operate in 
dynamic and unpredictable environments. 
Dual-task training can improve balance and 
coordination while performing cognitive 
tasks, such as the Stroop test. Penko et al. 
(2021) found significant improvements in 
both cognitive and motor performance 
under dual-task conditions, underscoring its 
possible utility in physically demanding 
scenarios. For military personnel recovering 
from mild traumatic brain injury (mTBI), 
dual-task training provides a critical means 
of assessing and possibly improving 
readiness to return to duty. Scherer et al. 
(2013) emphasized that dual-task training 
may address subtle cognitive and 
sensorimotor deficits, which are often 
missed in single-task assessments, 
enhancing operational competence and 
recovery outcomes. 
Military operations often require individuals 
to manage multiple tasks under high-stress 
conditions. Dual-task training may 
enhance stress resilience by improving the 
capacity to perform cognitive tasks while 
engaging in physically demanding activities. 
Vine et al. (2024) noted that soldiers trained 
under dual-task conditions demonstrated 
superior task performance and decision-
making under stress compared to those 
trained in single-task environments. 
Moreover, dual-task training can have a 
significant impact on specific executive 
functions. Improved working memory 
performance is evidenced by better results 
on tests like the 2-back test immediately 
after dual-task interventions (Zhang et al., 
2023). Additionally, dual-task training can 
enhance coordination skills necessary for 
managing cognitive-motor interference, a 
benefit less evident in single-task training 
(Schubert et al., 2017; Strobach et al., 
2014). Enhanced attentional control and 
inhibitory processes may enable soldiers 
to better suppress irrelevant information and 
focus on critical stimuli (Strobach, 2020). 
Emerging evidence from neuroscience 
highlights how neural efficiency, or the 
ability to execute complex tasks with fewer 
cognitive resources, can contribute to the

effectiveness of dual-task training. 
Functional imaging studies reveal that 
individuals who engage in dual-task 
exercises may show improved activation in 
areas such as the prefrontal cortex, which 
is critical for anticipation, decision-
making, and motor coordination (Yarrow 
et al., 2009). These neural adaptations may 
allow military personnel to maintain clarity 
and precision in high-pressure situations, 
optimizing their operational readiness and 
resilience. 
Additional findings suggest that dual-task 
training protocols that incorporate rapid 
decision-making under dynamic and 
unpredictable conditions may improve 
reaction time, situational awareness, and 
response precision (Beurskens et al., 
2021). These elements are particularly 
critical for military personnel who must 
adapt to evolving threats and environments. 
Similarly, dual-task exercises that include 
elements of spatial awareness and timing 
may further support motor control and 
tactical execution (Gattoni et al., 2023). 
The potential benefits of dual-task training 
can extend beyond the specific tasks 
practiced. Worden and Vallis (2014) 
demonstrated that dual-task training 
protocols may improve performance in both 
cognitive and motor domains more 
effectively than single-task training, 
suggesting that this approach prepares 
personnel for the multitasking demands of 
military operations. Reactive agility drills, 
where participants respond to dynamic and 
unpredictable stimuli while performing 
physical tasks, serve as a prime example. 
Such drills mimic real-world operational 
scenarios, potentially enhancing reaction 
time, situational awareness, and 
decision-making under uncertainty 
(Serpell et al., 2011). Training under 
cognitive load or fatigue conditions, as 
suggested by the Challenge Point 
Framework, may further prepare personnel 
to sustain high performance even under 
extreme stress and physical exertion 
(Guadagnoli & Lee, 2004). 
Dual-task training interventions typically 
involve the simultaneous practice of 
cognitive and motor tasks, whereas 
single-task interventions focus on one 
domain at a time. Common dual-task 
training methods include combining 
cognitive tasks, such as serial subtractions 
or visual search tasks, with motor tasks, 
such as balance maintenance or obstacle 
navigation (Beurskens et al., 2021). 
Participants may be instructed to prioritize 
either the cognitive or motor task during 
practice, though improvements are typically 
observed regardless of prioritization 
(Beurskens et al., 2020). Another method 
involves navigating dynamic obstacles 
while responding to cognitive challenges, 
training multitasking capabilities (Worden 
& Vallis, 2014). 
Dual-task training can be a valuable tool for 
military personnel, potentially enhancing 
cognitive-motor performance, postural 
stability, recovery post-TBI, and 
resilience under stress. By addressing the 
multitasking requirements of military 
operations and training executive 
functions such as working memory, 
cognitive flexibility, and attention, dual-
task training may contribute to improved 
readiness and operational competence.
